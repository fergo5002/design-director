# Slop and language

Slop is design nobody decided. A language is design somebody decided, for this subject,
and could defend line by line. They can use the same typeface and the same grid. The
difference is whether each choice has a reason that belongs to the brief.

The tells change every year. The tests below do not.

## Five tests

Run them on every direction before you show it, and on every reference before it goes
in a domain's library.

1. **The swap test.** Replace the name and logo with a competitor's. If nothing else
   on the page would need to change, nothing on the page was designed for this one.
2. **The reason test.** Point at any choice (the face, the accent, the radius, the
   way the hero moves) and say why it is that and not the obvious alternative. "It
   looks clean" and "it is modern" are not reasons. "The menu is set like a hotel
   ledger because the owners still keep one" is.
3. **The subject test.** Can you trace the palette, the imagery and the voice back to
   the subject's own world (its materials, its place, its people, its trade)? Colour
   that came from a gradient generator fails. Colour that came from the stone the
   cottages are built of passes.
4. **The intensity test.** Generated work is evenly loud: every section gets an icon,
   a heading, a card and an animation. Designed work spends its boldness in one place
   and keeps the rest quiet. If you cannot name the one memorable thing, there is none.
5. **The date test.** Would this have looked exactly the same on a template site in
   any year from 2021 to now? If the answer is yes, it is the average of the internet,
   which is what a model produces when nobody steers it.

A direction that fails two of these goes back. One failure needs a reason to stay.

## What slop looks like, by layer

- **Layout.** Centred hero, two buttons, a row of logos, three icon cards, a
  testimonial carousel, a pricing table, a gradient call-to-action band, a four-column
  footer. Bento grids of unrelated tiles. Every section the same height and padding.
- **Type.** The default sans at every size, display set at the body's tracking, a
  tracked uppercase label (an eyebrow) above every heading, gradient text.
- **Colour.** Purple to blue, teal to violet, a single saturated accent on everything
  clickable, dark mode with neon glows that no brand asked for.
- **Imagery.** Stock people smiling at laptops, 3D blobs and glass shapes, isometric
  illustration, AI renders with soft impossible light, emoji standing in for icons.
- **Components.** Glass cards on an aurora, `rounded-2xl` and a drop shadow on
  everything, pill badges on every value, a toast for every action, browser-default
  form controls on an otherwise styled page.
- **Motion.** Everything fades up on scroll by the same 20px, at the same speed, in
  the same order. Parallax for its own sake. Loaders that exist to look busy.
- **Copy.** "Seamless", "unlock", "elevate", "empower", "your journey", "in today's
  fast-paced world". Three adjectives in a row. Headings that are slogans with no
  noun the reader can picture. A paragraph that explains why the page exists.

## Convention is not slop

Every category has conventions that people rely on: a rate shown next to the loan
amount, availability on a calendar before the price, a phone number in the header of a
local business, the menu one tap away on a restaurant site. Breaking those costs trust
and is not originality. Each domain dossier keeps two lists for this reason:
**conventions to keep** (do them unusually well) and **conventions to break** (habits
that are lazy or dated). Slop is the decoration a model borrows from everywhere,
never the structure a category earned.

## What a language is

A visual language is a small set of rules that come from the subject and are applied
with discipline:

- **A source.** One or two things in the subject's world the language is drawn from:
  a material, a place, a trade's own documents, a period, a person.
- **Few rules, held hard.** One or two typefaces with a reason, a palette that is
  mostly one colour family, one way of handling imagery, one motion idea. Fewer rules
  applied everywhere beats many rules applied once.
- **Range inside the rules.** The quiet parts are genuinely quiet so the one
  memorable thing can be loud.
- **Specificity in the copy.** Names, numbers, places, the actual product. A real
  detail does more than any adjective.
- **It would be wrong for another brief.** If the language would suit a different
  client just as well, it is a theme, not a language.

## Dated is a kind of slop

Good moves wear out. Glassmorphism was fresh in 2021 and is wallpaper now. When you
add a reference to a domain, note the year of the work. When you audit a domain,
retire references whose signature move has since become a template (`ddr domain ref
retire <slug> <id> --reason "..."`). A library that keeps worn-out exemplars teaches
the next job to make worn-out work.
