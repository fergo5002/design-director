# Research for a new brand

Brand creation needs extensive exploration, unexpected
combinations and repeated visual iteration. A shallow search followed by a polished
default is a failed process. Apply this to new identities and substantial brand
reinventions, not to routine component edits.

## Build a library that changes the design

Start with the brief's fixed constraints and one question about the real creative
forks. Research widely before settling. Move across independent disciplines:
award-winning websites and their actual interactions; graphic studios and identity
case studies; independent type foundries and specimens; photography and materials;
art, archives and culturally relevant objects; creative coding and motion; critical
discussion about what succeeds and what has become a cliche.

Use current award lists to discover work, then visit the actual work. Read the
designer's own explanation when available. Search summaries and gallery thumbnails
are leads, not evidence of an interaction. Inspect desktop and phone compositions.
Do not describe a loading screen as the finished design. Record failed access.

Keep a project-local reference ledger. For each useful reference record the exact
URL, creator, access date, inspection depth (search, article, visual, interaction or
source), the specific technique, its relevance to this brief, what to leave behind,
and the asset or licence status. A list of links without decisions is not research.
Distinguish observed technique from an inference about how it was implemented.

Depth is measured by changed decisions and tested combinations, not by a fixed
number of links. For an explicitly extensive exploration, keep going beyond the
first plausible direction. Look for references which contradict it. Do not promise
that research proves global originality or guarantees the user's approval.

## Combine mechanisms, then make something

Extract separate ingredients: composition, letterform, material, movement, image
treatment and interaction. Combine ingredients from unrelated sources, held
together by one idea relevant to the brand. Random selection can break a stale
pattern, but the designer must curate the result. A reproducible shuffle of the
reference library is useful when it produces a visible new experiment.

Borrow techniques and principles. Do not repackage a recognisable competitor logo,
wordmark, copyrighted photograph or paid font as the new brand. Use original marks,
licensed images, clearly labelled generated art or verified public-domain material.
Put source attribution beside research imagery. A four-letter wordmark is custom
lettering, not a finished typeface. Record that distinction.

## Iterate in the browser

Make genuinely different directions in layout, image treatment and motion, not a
single template with palette switches. Prototype the defining interaction early.
An identity that depends on motion needs an equally recognisable static state.
Test the mark at icon size, in one colour, as large artwork and beside real UI.

Inspect each round. Write down the visible weakness, the change made and what the
next inspection showed. Return to the library if the result is generic, cluttered,
illegible or only interesting because it resembles a reference. Preserve meaningful
rejected experiments and their reasons. Passing technical checks does not prove
beauty. Mark the strongest direction as a recommendation pending the client's judgement.

Deliver an experience, with the research attached: a usable HTML concept or other
requested format, working interactions, actual type samples, usable image assets,
logo explorations and the source ledger. Keep the first view visual and concise;
put the deeper rationale and licensing detail within reach. State the scope of
browser checks and any untested production, device or licensing questions.
